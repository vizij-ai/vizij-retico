\title{Rendering Incremental Dialogue on an Expressive Face:\\
A retico--Vizij Bridge for Multimodal HRI}

\IEEEauthorblockA{Semio\\
saad@semio.community}

\begin{abstract}
Spoken dialogue with an embodied agent is not a text exchange. Humans coordinate
conversation through a dense layer of non-verbal, incremental behavior: gaze that
holds and averts, brows that anticipate a turn, nods and continuers that land
\emph{during} a partner's utterance, and a face that carries the affect of what is
being said. Most conversational stacks discard this layer, because the two
capabilities needed to keep it live in different systems: incremental dialogue
processing, and real-time expressive rendering.
We present \textbf{vizij-retico}, a bridge that connects the retico incremental
spoken-dialogue framework to Vizij, a real-time expressive 3D face, through a
single WebSocket protocol that preserves incremental unit (IU) semantics over the
wire and an \emph{editable} IU$\rightarrow$animation mapping. The mapping is data
rather than code: channel targets, timings, gaze arbitration priorities, and
emotion blends are declarative entries a practitioner can retune without touching
driver logic. We describe the six behaviors it renders --- turn-taking posture,
backchannels, nods, lip-sync, affect, and gaze --- and the arbitration policy that
keeps them from fighting over the same face.
\todo{one results sentence, once section V is measured}
The bridge is a portable expressive layer: the same value stream targets a
physical robot face (Quori).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% Target: ~0.75 column. Sources: docs/01.
% =====================================================================

Conversation is incremental and multimodal. We look at a partner while they speak
and glance away while planning a reply; we nod and murmur to signal that we are
following; we begin to react to an utterance before it finishes; our faces carry
affect that colors the words. Agents that wait for a complete utterance and then
answer with text or speech alone discard all of it, and the result feels latent,
flat, and socially inert.

The workshop asks directly whether language is a sufficient modality for
human--robot communication, and what other modalities can help. We take the
question literally and answer it with a system. The two capabilities required are
mature, but they live in separate worlds. \textbf{Incremental dialogue
processing} --- emitting partial and revisable signals in real time, so that the
system knows not only ``the user said $X$'' but ``the user is \emph{still}
speaking,'' ``a turn shift is likely within 400\,ms,'' ``this is a good moment to
backchannel'' --- is the domain of retico~\cite{michael2020retico}, built on the
incremental unit model~\cite{schlangen2011iu}. \textbf{Real-time expressive
rendering} --- a face driven continuously on many channels at once: gaze, eyelids,
brow, jaw, per-viseme mouth shapes, emotion blends --- is the domain of Vizij.
Neither knows about the other. retico has no viseme module and no transport to a
renderer; Vizij has no notion of incremental dialogue.

That gap is the contribution. This paper describes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item a \textbf{bridge} that fans heterogeneous IU streams into one WebSocket
        protocol which preserves \textsc{add}/\textsc{revoke}/\textsc{commit}
        semantics end to end (\S\ref{sec:protocol});
  \item an \textbf{editable IU$\rightarrow$animation mapping} --- the intellectual
        core --- that specifies channels, verbs, timings, and priorities as data
        (\S\ref{sec:mapping});
  \item a \textbf{gaze arbitration policy} that lets six behaviors share one face
        without conflict, including the posture/impulse split that permits a nod
        during sustained mutual gaze (\S\ref{sec:arbitration});
  \item a running, deployed system with runtime-swappable perception and
        generation backends, and an honest account of where the rendering is
        constrained by the rig rather than by the design (\S\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
% Target: ~0.75 column.
% =====================================================================

\textbf{Incremental dialogue.} The incremental unit
model~\cite{schlangen2011iu} decomposes dialogue processing into modules that
exchange typed units with \textsc{add}, \textsc{revoke}, and \textsc{commit}
semantics, so a consumer may react to a hypothesis and revise gracefully when it
changes. Latency to first reaction is then bounded by the \emph{signal} rather
than the \emph{utterance}. retico~\cite{michael2020retico} is an open-source
realization with interchangeable ASR, TTS, LLM, and vision backends.

\textbf{Turn-taking and listener signals.} Voice Activity Projection
(VAP)~\cite{ekstedt2022vap} continuously predicts the near-future voice activity
of both parties from audio alone, giving a running probability of an imminent
turn shift; \cite{skantze2021review} surveys the broader problem in
conversational systems and HRI. \todo{cite the MaAI toolkit --- verify the exact
reference for retico-maai's VAP/backchannel/nod predictors before submission}
Crucially these are \emph{streams}, not end-of-turn decisions, which is what
makes rendering them mid-utterance possible at all.

\textbf{Expressive agent and robot faces.} \todo{Furhat/Skantze, situated
dialogue (Marge), social robot faces (Irfan) --- 3--4 citations, panelist-aware}

\textbf{Position.} Prior work does incrementality \emph{or} expressive
rendering. We contribute the glue that makes incremental signals drive a face
coherently, and we contribute it as reusable infrastructure rather than as a
one-off demo: the mapping is an artifact a practitioner edits, not a behavior
tree we hard-coded.

\subsection{Architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the running system. A browser captures
microphone audio at 16\,kHz and streams PCM frames over a WebSocket. On the
backend, a retico network fans that audio out to two consumers in
\emph{parallel}: the turn-taking model and the recognizer. This is deliberate ---
it is why a turn-shift signal can reach the face before a transcript exists.
Transcripts pass through a gate (\S\ref{sec:barge}) to a streaming LLM, whose
output is split into clauses; each clause is synthesized and shipped to the
browser as it is produced, rather than after the reply completes. An affect
module derives the agent's intended emotion from the same generated text and
emits it as a first-class IU.

Every perception and generation stage is a swappable provider chosen at runtime
from the UI: recognition (browser Web Speech, Google Cloud STT, local Whisper),
turn-taking (VAP or reply-immediately), model, synthesis, voice, and facial
expression recognition. This matters for a systems paper in two ways: it makes
controlled comparisons cheap, and it forces the protocol to be the only contract
between the halves.

\subsection{The IU$\rightarrow$animation mapping}
\label{sec:mapping}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{02_mapping.pdf}
  \caption{IU $\rightarrow$ protocol event $\rightarrow$ face channel.}
  \label{fig:mapping}
\end{figure}

The mapping is the policy that turns discrete, incremental, sometimes-revised
dialogue signals into smooth, well-timed, non-conflicting facial behavior. It
lives in one editable module that the driver interprets; the driver itself is
generic. Five principles shape it:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Smoothed by default.} Expressive changes are tweens, never
        instantaneous writes. Direct writes are reserved for resets.
  \item \textbf{Revisable.} Applications are tracked by IU id, so a
        \textsc{revoke} can cancel or reverse a tween still in flight. Impulses
        that have already played are not undone.
  \item \textbf{Arbitrated.} Shared channels obey a fixed priority; pose weights
        compose but are capped.
  \item \textbf{Rig-agnostic.} Targets are semantic keys resolved against the
        live rig's declared inputs at load time. A channel the rig lacks disables
        its entry with a warning; the rest of the mapping still runs, so
        capabilities degrade independently.
  \item \textbf{Tunable in one place.} Durations, easings, amplitudes,
        thresholds, and priorities are fields, not magic numbers in the driver.
\end{enumerate}

Concretely: \texttt{turn.state} selects a posture (gaze target, brow raise,
optional blink, tween duration) from a table keyed by state;
\texttt{backchannel.cue} fires a brow flash of 110\,ms up and 240\,ms down;
\texttt{nod.cue} runs a scripted oscillation; \texttt{emotion.affect} and
\texttt{emotion.fer} resolve an emotion label to a \emph{blend} of base pose
weights; \texttt{speech.audio} drives the mouth.

The emotion blend deserves a note. The rig ships six legible base poses (happy,
sad, anger, surprise, concerned, sleepy). Rather than restrict the agent to
those, the mapping expresses everything else as a mixture --- fear as
$0.6\,\textrm{surprise} + 0.5\,\textrm{concern}$, confusion as
$0.6\,\textrm{concern} + 0.35\,\textrm{surprise}$ --- with an intensity scalar
over the whole blend. Mirroring the user's affect is deliberately scaled to
$0.45$: at full strength it reads as mimicry rather than empathy.

\subsection{Gaze arbitration}
\label{sec:arbitration}

Gaze is the most contended resource on a face: turn posture, aversion, joint
attention, and idle all want the eyes. A single slot is held by the highest-
priority active intent,
\[
  \textrm{joint\_attention} > \textrm{aversion} > \textrm{turn} > \textrm{idle},
\]
and when the top intent clears, the arbiter recomputes from what remains and
animates to the winner. Idle is the floor: micro-saccades and spontaneous blinks
resume whenever nothing else is active. We found the spontaneous blink to be
non-optional --- cue-driven blinks are sparse by design, and with the rig's own
idle animation disabled so it cannot fight the driver, a face without one simply
stares.

The load-bearing detail is the \textbf{posture/impulse split}. Nods and
backchannels are impulses that ride \emph{on top of} the current gaze posture
without claiming the slot. This is what lets the agent nod at a clause boundary
while holding mutual gaze, instead of a nod cancelling the attention it is meant
to express. It is expressed in the mapping as which channels an entry claims, so
the arbitration policy is editable along with everything else.

\subsection{Lip-sync}

retico's TTS emits audio, not phoneme timing, so lip-sync is a gap the bridge
fills. Two paths: where the synthesizer returns speech
marks, phoneme-timed viseme poses are driven directly; where it does not, jaw
opening follows playback amplitude. The second is worse and we label it as the
fallback rather than dressing it up.

The viseme timeline holds one counter-intuitive result worth reporting, because
it cost us a rewrite to find. A first implementation cross-faded a fixed 55\,ms
window starting \emph{at} each phoneme's timestamp, on a clock captured before
the audio had decoded. The mouth popped between shapes. Three separate causes
compounded: every shape arrived late, the clock drifted from the audio and never
re-synced, and each pose reached full weight before the next began. The fix
anticipates --- the cross-fade \emph{starts} early enough that full weight lands
\emph{on} the phoneme --- reads the audio element's own clock every frame, and,
critically, gives each tween roughly four times longer than the interval until
the next phoneme re-targets it. Weights therefore never saturate, peaking around
20--40\%, and because each tween re-targets from the current live value the net
effect is a first-order low-pass filter on the viseme signal. Correct-duration
tweens produce crisp, articulate, and markedly more jarring lip-sync. Smoothness
here is not a rendering nicety; it is the difference between speech and a
slideshow.

\subsection{Barge-in without deafness}
\label{sec:barge}

An agent that streams speech will hear itself. The obvious guard --- mute
recognition while speaking --- also destroys barge-in, and in our first
implementation it destroyed listening outright: the mute window was extended and
never reset. We replaced it with \textbf{echo rejection} on text. Transcripts
keep flowing while the agent speaks; the gate drops one only when it
substantially overlaps what the agent is currently saying. A non-overlapping
transcript arriving mid-reply is treated as a barge-in: queued speech is
cancelled, the browser is told to stop playback, and the turn is processed.
Decisions are made per utterance, never per fragment, so a dropped
\textsc{commit} cannot leave stale words to be prepended to the user's next turn.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}
% Target: ~0.25 column. Be direct; reviewers reward it.
% =====================================================================

\textbf{Screen versus physical embodiment.} The face is rendered in a browser.
The same value stream targets a physical robot face (Quori), and the mapping is
structured so that only channel resolution changes; that port is future work,
not a claim.

\textbf{Head motion is composited, not rigged.} Nods, shakes, and tilts are
applied as a transform on the element wrapping the face canvas rather than
through the rig. This is worth stating plainly because the obvious diagnosis is
wrong: the rig \emph{does} expose a whole-head transform node, but only as a
computed value. The writable override inputs are not surfaced by the current
runtime build, and writing the computed path holds only until the graph
re-evaluates. The rig-native path opens as soon as the runtime exposes those
inputs; one table and one function in the mapping would change.

\textbf{We render predictions, we do not improve them.} Nod and backchannel
timing quality is entirely inherited from the upstream predictors.

\textbf{Affect is declared, not perceived.} The agent's expression comes from a
tag the language model emits ahead of its reply, with keyword inference as a
fallback for models that ignore the instruction. It is the agent's
\emph{intended} affect. The camera path that would perceive the user's is
implemented but coarse: categorical rather than dimensional, and noisy enough to
need hysteresis.

\textbf{No user study.} Every claim here is a systems claim.
